Hair changes as we get older. It gets finer, the parting widens, the colour shifts. For many women there comes a point when a wig or a topper is simply the easiest way to have hair they like again.

Nothing about choosing a wig is different at 65 from at 35. But a few things tend to matter more.

## Comfort comes first

**A light cap.** You'll wear it for hours. Alopecia UK's guide recommends lightweight caps that put breathability and comfort first, and that's good advice at any age.

**A soft inside.** Skin gets thinner and more sensitive with age. Hand-tied caps and monofilament tops are gentler than basic wefts. See [cap constructions explained](/blogs/guides/wig-cap-constructions-explained).

**Easy to put on.** Look for ear tabs you can feel and adjusters that are simple to work. If your hands are stiff, hook adjusters can be fiddly. Velcro tabs are easier.

**The right size.** With less hair underneath, an average cap can feel loose. If yours slips even when tightened, try a petite. See [how to measure](/blogs/guides/how-to-measure-your-head-for-a-wig).

## Colour

**Go softer, not darker.** The colour you wore at 40 can look hard against your skin now. Macmillan's general advice, your own shade or slightly lighter, applies doubly.

**Don't be afraid of grey.** A good grey blend looks modern and suits your skin, because it's the colour your hair has chosen. See [grey and silver wigs](/blogs/guides/grey-and-silver-wigs).

**Choose a blend.** A mix of tones looks like hair. A single flat colour looks like a wig.

## Style

There are no rules about age and length. Wear what you like. But some things make life easier:

- **Short to chin-length styles** are the lightest, the coolest and the least work. They also last longest.
- **Soft layers round the face** flatter more than a blunt, heavy cut.
- **A fringe** hides the front edge of the wig and softens the forehead.
- **Not too much volume.** Macmillan suggests the same volume as your own hair or a little less.

A hairdresser used to wigs can trim any style to suit you.

## Easy care

Synthetic is the practical choice. You wash it, leave it on a stand overnight and it's ready. No blow-drying, no setting. See [how to wash a synthetic wig](/blogs/guides/how-to-wash-a-synthetic-wig).

Two wigs worn in turn means you're never caught out on wash day.

## Would a topper do?

If your hair is thin on top but still reasonable at the sides and back, a topper may be all you need. It's lighter than a wig and you keep your own hairline.

The catch is the clips, which need sound hair to hold. Jon Renau warns that the wrong size can pull on your own hair and cause more loss. If your hair is fine all over, a lightweight wig is kinder. See [wig or topper?](/blogs/guides/thinning-hair-wig-or-topper)

## Glasses and hearing aids

Both sit where a wig's ear tabs sit. Put the wig on first, then your glasses, and try the arms both over and under the hair at the sides to see which is more comfortable. With hearing aids, check the wig doesn't press on them or rub the microphone. A fitter can help you find a cap that sits comfortably with both.

## Help with the cost

**VAT relief.** Age on its own doesn't qualify. HMRC's guidance says the relief doesn't cover someone who is elderly but not chronically sick or disabled. But if your hair loss comes from a medical condition or treatment, you may be able to buy without VAT. See [VAT relief on wigs](/blogs/guides/vat-relief-on-wigs).

**NHS wigs.** Available through a hospital consultant if you have a medical cause. If you get Pension Credit Guarantee Credit, an NHS wig in England is free. See [NHS wigs](/blogs/guides/nhs-wigs-who-qualifies-and-how-to-get-one).

## If thinning is new or sudden

Mention it to your GP. Hair does thin with age, but sudden or patchy loss can have a cause that's worth checking.
