Nobody tells you how many decisions there are. Fibre, cap, size, colour, length, and that's before you've looked at a price. It's a lot, particularly if you're buying because your hair is thinning or about to go, and you'd rather not be doing this at all.

So here's the short version. There are five choices that matter, and if you take them in order the rest falls into place.

## 1. What the hair is made of

Most first wigs are synthetic, and that's no bad thing. Synthetic hair holds its style, so you wash it, let it dry overnight and it's ready to wear. Human hair can be styled like your own, but it costs far more and needs styling every time.

A higher price doesn't always buy a more natural look either. Cancer Research UK makes the point that some cheaper wigs look very realistic.

Read more in [synthetic vs human hair vs heat-friendly](/blogs/guides/synthetic-vs-human-hair-vs-heat-friendly-wigs).

## 2. How the cap is made

The cap is the part you'll feel all day. A basic wefted cap is light and airy. A monofilament top shows what looks like scalp at the parting. A lace front gives you a soft, believable hairline, which matters if you want your hair off your face.

If your scalp is tender or you have no hair underneath, the softer hand-tied caps are worth the extra. [Cap constructions explained](/blogs/guides/wig-cap-constructions-explained) goes through each one.

## 3. Your size

Most women are an average, but not all. Two minutes with a tape measure saves you a wig that slips or pinches. See [how to measure your head](/blogs/guides/how-to-measure-your-head-for-a-wig).

If you're expecting to lose your hair, Macmillan suggests a wig that adjusts, because you'll need a smaller fit once your hair has gone.

## 4. Colour

For a first wig, stay close to home. Macmillan's advice is your own colour or a shade lighter. Many women also find a rooted shade, one that's slightly darker at the roots, looks less "wiggy" than a single flat colour.

Read more in [how to choose a wig colour](/blogs/guides/how-to-choose-a-wig-colour).

## 5. Style and length

Pick something near what you wear now, or a style you've worn and liked. Macmillan suggests matching the volume of your own hair or going slightly less, since too much hair is what gives a wig away. Any hairdresser who's used to wigs can thin and trim it for you afterwards.

## What will it cost?

Macmillan puts synthetic wigs at around £50 to several hundred pounds, and human hair at a few hundred to a few thousand. Our [UK price guide](/blogs/guides/how-much-do-wigs-cost-in-the-uk) breaks that down.

Before you pay full price, check two things:

- **NHS wigs.** Free in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, and from £80.15 in England. See [NHS wigs](/blogs/guides/nhs-wigs-who-qualifies-and-how-to-get-one).
- **VAT relief.** If your hair loss is down to a medical condition or treatment, you may not have to pay VAT. See [VAT relief on wigs](/blogs/guides/vat-relief-on-wigs).

## Where to buy

You can buy in a shop with a fitter, or online. Both work. [Online vs in a shop](/blogs/guides/buying-a-wig-online-vs-in-a-shop) covers what to expect from each.

## Give it a week

Almost everyone finds a wig strange at first. It feels bigger than it looks. Wear it around the house, then with someone you trust, then out. Macmillan suggests taking a friend when you choose, too. An honest second opinion is worth having.

And if the first one isn't right, that's normal. Most women learn more from their first wig than from any guide, this one included.
