Alopecia changes what you need from a wig. When there's little or no hair underneath, there's nothing for the cap to hold on to and nothing between the cap and your skin. So grip and softness count for more than they would for anyone else.

For many women with alopecia it also isn't a few months. It's every day, perhaps for years. That changes the sums on cost and comfort as well.

## The cap matters most

Alopecia UK's advice is to look for a cap that's lightweight and puts breathability and comfort first.

- **Hand-tied caps** are the softest against bare skin, with no ridges from wefts.
- **Double monofilament tops** put a smooth layer between the knots and your scalp.
- **Wefted caps** are the airiest. Alopecia UK's guide describes them as light, with extra air circulation, and good for a sensitive scalp.

Our guide to [cap constructions](/blogs/guides/wig-cap-constructions-explained) explains each.

Fit matters more too. A cap that's slightly big will slide on a smooth scalp, so [measure carefully](/blogs/guides/how-to-measure-your-head-for-a-wig) and look at petite sizes if you're at the small end of average.

## Keeping it on

Without hair to grip, most women use one or more of these:

- **Wig tape or adhesive pads.** Cancer Research UK mentions adhesive pads for when all your hair has gone. Macmillan recommends hypoallergenic double-sided tape made for wigs.
- **A wig grip band.** A soft band worn under the wig that stops it sliding.
- **Caps with built-in grip.** Some caps have silicone or similar strips inside, made for total hair loss.

Try any tape on a small patch of skin first. And use Macmillan's check before you go out: bend forward, shake your head gently, and see if anything moves.

## Wearing one all day, every day

- **Give your scalp a rest.** Take the wig off at home when you can.
- **Think about a liner.** A thin cotton one helps if the cap rubs, though it can make the wig shift.
- **Have more than one.** Two wigs worn in turn last longer than one worn daily, and you're never stuck on wash day.
- **Plan for replacements.** A synthetic wig worn daily lasts months. Budget for that from the start.

## Getting a wig through the NHS

This is where it gets uneven, and it helps to know that before you ask.

Wigs for alopecia usually come through a hospital dermatologist rather than your GP. What you're offered depends heavily on where you live. Alopecia UK says it regularly hears of some patients getting wigs on prescription while others can't, and that often nobody in the NHS mentions the option unless you raise it. So raise it.

**What it costs**

- In **Scotland and Wales**, wigs on NHS prescription are free.
- In **England**, a standard synthetic NHS wig is £80.15 unless you're exempt. See [NHS wigs](/blogs/guides/nhs-wigs-who-qualifies-and-how-to-get-one).

**What to ask**

Alopecia UK suggests asking what the wig is actually worth. If the charge gets you a wig that would cost around £200, that's a good deal. If it gets you one you could buy for less yourself, it isn't.

Ask as well how many wigs you can have each year. It varies. One hospital trust, for example, tells patients their prescription covers two wigs meant to last a year. Yours may be more generous or less.

Alopecia UK, with the British Association of Dermatologists and others, has published a Charter for Best Practice for NHS Wig Provision. If your local service falls short, it's worth mentioning when you speak to your dermatology team.

## Buying privately

You may not have to pay VAT. The relief is for people who are chronically sick or disabled, and wigs are among the goods covered. You make a declaration when you buy. Read [VAT relief on wigs](/blogs/guides/vat-relief-on-wigs) before your first order, because you can't claim it afterwards.

## Support

**Alopecia UK** is the place to start. It has information on every type of alopecia, a guide to wigs, support groups, and advice on speaking up for yourself with the NHS. Its website also points to a wig bank that recycles wigs people no longer need.
