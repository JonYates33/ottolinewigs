Many Black women know far more about wigs than any guide could teach. Wigs are part of everyday styling, a way to protect natural hair and change a look.

Needing one because of hair loss is different. You want hair that looks like yours, your texture and your density, and the mainstream medical wig ranges haven't always offered that. This guide points to the help that exists in the UK.

## Specialist help

**Cancer Hair Care.** A UK hair loss charity offering free advice before, during and after cancer treatment. Cancer Research UK notes that it has specialist advice for people with Afro-textured hair, and an Afro hair guide to chemotherapy hair loss. Its helpline is 0300 102 2525.

**Cancer Black Care.** Macmillan suggests contacting them for support and advice.

**Cancer And Afro Hair.** St George's Hospital lists this as a trichology-led service for cancer-related hair loss that specialises in Afro-textured hair.

**Your hospital.** Provision is improving in places. Guy's and St Thomas' in London, for example, offers free wigs and scalp treatments on referral for patients with Afro hair who have alopecia or are having cancer treatment, and says most of the wigs from its supplier are suitable. Ask your own hospital team what's on offer and say plainly what you need. If the range doesn't include your texture, say so. It's how services change.

## What to look for in a wig

**Texture first.** Coily, curly, kinky-straight, relaxed-look. A wig in your own texture is the one you'll feel like yourself in. Take photos of your hair as you wear it to any fitting.

**Density.** Many ready-made wigs are either very full or fine. Look for one close to your own, and remember a stylist can thin a wig that's too heavy.

**The parting and lace.** On lace-front and monofilament wigs, the lace or mesh shows as "scalp". Ask what shades it comes in and whether it can be tinted to your skin tone.

**Fibre.** Human hair can be styled with heat and blends best with your own edges. Good textured synthetic costs less and keeps its curl. See [synthetic vs human hair](/blogs/guides/synthetic-vs-human-hair-vs-heat-friendly-wigs).

**Fit.** If you have a lot of hair underneath, braided flat, you may need a larger cap. If you've lost your hair, you may need a smaller one than you're used to. [Measure](/blogs/guides/how-to-measure-your-head-for-a-wig) as you'll wear it.

## Protecting the hair you have

This matters, because wigs can harm as well as protect.

Trichologist Lorna Jones describes traction alopecia as by far the commonest type of non-scarring hair loss in women with Afro-textured hair, caused by tight styles including braids, tight ponytails, wigs and locs.

The Wimpole Clinic makes a similar point: wigs can protect fragile Afro-textured hair from daily damage, but ill-fitting, heavy or adhesive-secured wigs may raise the risk of traction alopecia.

So, especially if your hair is already thinning:

- Keep braids or cornrows underneath loose, not tight.
- Avoid glue along your hairline. A grip band or a well-fitted cap is gentler.
- Don't wear the same tight style under a wig for weeks on end.
- Take the wig off at night and let your scalp breathe.
- Don't anchor combs or clips into fragile edges.

## If your hair loss isn't from treatment

See your GP or a dermatologist, and ask about a referral to someone who knows Afro-textured hair. Lorna Jones notes that one condition, CCCA, is the commonest type of permanent hair loss in Black women. Some kinds of hair loss can be slowed or stopped if they're caught early, so it's worth being seen.
