If you're reading this, you've probably just been told your treatment may cause hair loss, and a wig is one more thing on a list that's already too long. This guide is here to make that one thing simpler.

Not every chemotherapy drug causes hair loss, and you don't have to wear a wig if it does. Plenty of women choose scarves, hats or nothing at all. Your nurse can tell you what to expect from your particular treatment.

## When to choose your wig

If you can face it, before your hair goes.

Macmillan gives three reasons. You can match the style and colour to your own hair. You get used to wearing it before you need it. And you're ready if your hair falls out sooner than you expected.

Cancer Research UK suggests taking photos of your hair as you usually wear it. If you decide later, a fitter can work from those.

There's no rush on the day, either. Macmillan is clear that you don't have to decide straight away, and suggests taking a friend or relative for an honest opinion.

## What to look for

**A cap that adjusts.** A wig fitted over a full head of hair will be loose once your hair has gone. Macmillan and Cancer Research UK both advise choosing one that tightens.

**Something soft inside.** Treatment can leave your scalp tender. Hand-tied caps and double monofilament tops are the gentlest. See [cap constructions explained](/blogs/guides/wig-cap-constructions-explained).

**Synthetic, most likely.** It's ready to wear after a wash, which matters on days when you're worn out, and it's what the NHS usually provides.

**A shade close to your own, or a touch lighter.** That's Macmillan's suggestion. Cancer Research UK adds that your complexion can change a little during treatment, so it's worth asking the fitter what will still suit you in a few months.

**No more hair than you have now.** Macmillan suggests the same volume as your own hair or slightly less.

## Getting one through the NHS

Start with your specialist nurse or the hospital's cancer information centre. Many hospitals arrange fittings themselves.

- In **Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland**, NHS wigs are free.
- In **England**, a standard synthetic NHS wig costs £80.15 unless you qualify for a free one. You do if you're a hospital inpatient, on certain benefits or a low income, or under 16 (or under 19 in full-time education).

Some hospitals give a voucher towards a wig from particular suppliers instead. It varies, so ask. Full details are in our [NHS wigs guide](/blogs/guides/nhs-wigs-who-qualifies-and-how-to-get-one).

## If you buy privately

You shouldn't have to pay VAT. Macmillan and Cancer Research UK both say wigs bought because of hair loss from cancer treatment are free of VAT, but you have to fill in the form when you buy. You can't claim it back afterwards, and not every seller offers it, so ask first. See [VAT relief on wigs](/blogs/guides/vat-relief-on-wigs).

## Comfort on a tender scalp

These are Macmillan's suggestions:

- Wear a thin cotton liner under the wig, and wash it daily. It may make the wig move a little, so you might need to try a couple.
- Freshen your scalp with a damp cloth, or an alcohol-free, fragrance-free spray.
- Take the wig off when you can: in the evening, at night and in the shower.
- If you want to use wig tape, use a hypoallergenic one made for wigs, and check with your doctor or nurse first, because treatment can make skin more sensitive.

## Keeping it safe

Keep an ordinary synthetic wig away from heat. Macmillan warns about flames, ovens and hairdryers, all of which can melt or frizz the fibre.

## When your hair comes back

You decide when to stop wearing it. Cancer Research UK notes that some women stop well before their hair is back to its old length, and that's fine.

## People who can help

- **Your hospital team.** They can refer you for a wig and tell you what you're entitled to.
- **Cancer Hair Care.** A charity giving free advice on hair loss, scalp cooling and hair care during treatment, including specialist advice for Afro-textured hair.
- **Look Good Feel Better.** Workshops and tutorials on managing changes in your appearance.
- **Macmillan.** Information, support and, in some cases, grants.
- **The Little Princess Trust.** Free human hair wigs for children and young people up to 24.
