Wig prices are all over the place, and it's hard to tell from a product photo why one costs £90 and the one next to it £900. Here's what the money is paying for.

## The broad picture

Macmillan gives these ranges for buying privately:

| Type | Typical price |
|---|---|
| Synthetic wig | around £50 to several hundred pounds |
| Human hair wig | a few hundred to a few thousand pounds |
| Wig made from your own hair | around £2,500 or more |

And on the NHS in England, from April 2026:

| NHS wig | Charge |
|---|---|
| Stock synthetic (modacrylic) | £80.15 |
| Partial human hair | £212.35 |
| Full bespoke human hair | £310.55 |

In Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, NHS wigs are free. Many women in England don't pay either. [Our NHS guide](/blogs/guides/nhs-wigs-who-qualifies-and-how-to-get-one) explains who.

## What pushes the price up

**The fibre.** Human hair costs several times what synthetic does.

**The cap.** Every part tied by hand takes time. A basic wefted cap is the cheapest, a monofilament top costs more, a lace front more again, and a fully hand-tied cap the most. See [cap constructions explained](/blogs/guides/wig-cap-constructions-explained).

**The length.** Longer means more hair, and with human hair the price climbs steeply.

**The brand.** The well-known names charge more, though you're generally getting better fibre and a wider choice of colours for it.

One thing price doesn't reliably buy is realism. Cancer Research UK says plainly that paying more doesn't guarantee a more natural look, and that some cheaper wigs look very convincing.

## The costs people forget

- **Replacing it.** A synthetic wig worn daily lasts months, not years. Two a year is a sensible thing to budget for.
- **Care products.** Wig shampoo and conditioner, a stand and a wide-tooth comb. Not expensive, but not optional.
- **Liners and grips.** A cotton liner or a wig grip, if you need them.
- **Cutting in.** A trim from a hairdresser who knows wigs makes a real difference to how natural it looks.
- **Upkeep on human hair.** Macmillan notes some need professional cleaning and styling, which can be costly.

## Six ways to pay less

1. **Ask about an NHS wig first.** Speak to your specialist nurse, consultant or dermatologist.
2. **Claim VAT relief if you're eligible.** If your hair loss is from a medical condition or cancer treatment, you may be able to buy without VAT. On a £240 wig that's £40 off. You have to claim when you buy, not afterwards. See [VAT relief on wigs](/blogs/guides/vat-relief-on-wigs).
3. **Ask your hospital about vouchers.** Some trusts give a voucher towards a wig from a set range. It varies a great deal from one hospital to the next.
4. **Look at charity help.** Cancer Research UK mentions that Macmillan gives one-off grants that can cover things like wigs. The Little Princess Trust provides free human hair wigs for children and young people up to 24.
5. **Try a wig bank.** These pass on wigs that are no longer needed, sometimes at a discount.
6. **Spend on the cap, save on the fibre.** A synthetic wig with a good cap often looks better than a human hair one with a basic cap, for much less.

## What should a first wig cost?

There's no right figure. But if you're buying a first synthetic wig privately, you don't need to go to the top of the range to get something comfortable that looks like you. Start in the middle, see what you like and don't like about it, and let that guide the next one.
